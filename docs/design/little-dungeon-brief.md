# The Little Dungeon: design brief

Written by Jason's wife and shared by Jason on 2026-10-02. This is the creative direction
for the game. Jason added: "I want a thread of randomness to add fun and replayability into
the game. Think procedurally generated level designs, storylines. Player feedback
incorporated into the storylines, choices that matter, roguelike gameplay elements and
experience/level up progressions to add a gamification element."

The text below is kept as written (lightly formatted for Markdown).

---

## Project vision

Build a playful, story-driven fantasy RPG for young children, beginning around
preschool/early elementary age.

The game should feel like a real fantasy adventure game, not an educational app with a
fantasy skin.

The child becomes an adventurer exploring an original magical kingdom filled with:
dungeons, dragons, knights, wizards, princesses and queens, magical creatures, treasure,
maps, quests, potions, dice, puzzles, hidden rooms, NPCs, choices and consequences.

The game should capture the spirit of tabletop fantasy RPGs without copying Dungeons &
Dragons intellectual property, terminology, characters, settings, artwork, text, or exact
rules. Create our own original world, characters, classes, creatures, terminology,
mechanics, artwork, and lore.

The central design principle:

> Learning is how the player interacts with the world.

The game should never feel like: "Stop playing and do a math problem."
Instead: "How do we open the dungeon door?" The answer happens to involve math.

## Core game loop

EXPLORE → DISCOVER → CHOOSE → LEARN → RESOLVE → REWARD → STORY

Example: the player discovers a magical door. The narrator says: "The door is protected by
an ancient pattern. Complete the pattern to unlock it." The child completes
🔴 🔵 🔴 🔵 ? The answer is 🔴. The door opens. The learning activity directly changes the
game world.

## Story-first design

Every learning activity should have a narrative reason to exist.

- **Math.** Not "Solve 3 + 2." Instead: "You have three magic stones. The bridge needs five.
  How many more do we need?"
- **Pre-writing.** Not "Trace this line." Instead: "Draw the magical path through the cave so
  your character can reach the crystal."
- **Colors.** Not "Identify blue." Instead: "The wizard needs the BLUE crystal for the potion."
- **Patterns.** Not "Complete the pattern." Instead: "The ancient door only opens when its
  magical symbols are placed in the correct order."
- **Reading.** Not "Read this word." Instead: "The sign says the treasure is under the BOX.
  Can you find the box?"
- **Map reading.** Not "Follow the directions." Instead: "The treasure is hidden in the red
  room. Use your map to find the way."
- **Storytelling.** Not "Answer a comprehension question." Instead: "The goblin is
  frightened. What should you say?"

## The dice system

Dice are an important mechanic, but they are only ONE learning mechanic. Use a simple
six-sided die initially. The die should be understandable and exciting for a young child.

- 1–2: something unexpected happens.
- 3–4: partial success or ordinary success.
- 5: strong success.
- 6: critical success / magical outcome.

IMPORTANT: do not make low rolls feel like punishment. A bad roll should create a funny,
interesting, or unexpected story event. Example: "You rolled a 2!" "The spell worked..."
"...but your hat is now stuck to the ceiling." The story continues.

## Math through RPG mechanics

Dice naturally introduce counting, number recognition, addition, subtraction, quantity
comparison, number relationships, and eventually simple multiplication/division.

Example: the character rolls 🎲 4. Character bonus: +2. Game asks: "What is 4 + 2?"
Answer: 6. The spell succeeds.

Later: "You found 7 potions." "You used 2." "How many are left?" The math is embedded
inside the adventure.

## Character attributes

Instead of complicated traditional RPG statistics, start with child-friendly attributes:

- ❤️ **Courage**: bravery and risk-taking.
- 🧠 **Cleverness**: math, logic, and problem solving.
- 📖 **Wisdom**: reading, clues, comprehension, and understanding.
- 💛 **Kindness**: helping characters and making compassionate choices.
- ✨ **Magic**: interacting with magical objects and spells.

Different learning activities can contribute to different attributes. The child's learning
progress therefore becomes part of their character progression.

## Character classes

Eventually allow children to choose an original fantasy class. Possible examples:

- ⚔️ **Knight**: courage, strength, and protection.
- 🧙 **Wizard**: magic, puzzles, patterns, and knowledge.
- 🏹 **Ranger**: exploration, observation, maps, and nature.
- 🛡️ **Guardian**: helping others, problem solving, and protecting companions.
- ✨ **Spellkeeper**: language, reading, storytelling, and magical knowledge.

These should be original concepts and names rather than reproductions of existing RPG class
systems.

## Learning system

The game should support multiple learning domains.

1. **Math.** Mechanics: dice, counting treasure, potion quantities, adding items, removing
   items, comparing quantities, simple arithmetic, number recognition. Example: "You have 3
   gold coins. Find 2 more."
2. **Pre-writing / fine motor.** Turn handwriting preparation into magical interaction.
   Progression: straight lines, curves, zigzags, loops, simple shapes, letters, simple
   words / name writing. Example: "Draw the magical path from the wizard to the crystal
   without touching the walls." The game evaluates the child's touchscreen movement. This
   should eventually help develop hand control, directionality, shape formation,
   visual-motor coordination and pre-writing skills.
3. **Patterns.** Patterns become magical locks. Examples: 🔴 🔵 🔴 🔵 ? and
   🟢 🟡 🟡 🟢 🟡 🟡 ? Eventually more complex sequences. Difficulty should increase
   gradually.
4. **Letters & phonics.** Ancient magical symbols/runes can represent letters. The game can
   introduce letter recognition, then letter sounds, initial sounds, matching sounds to
   letters, simple words, and early reading. Example: "The spell begins with the sound
   MMMMM." Child selects M. Eventually: "Which letter makes the first sound in MOON?"
5. **Reading.** Reading should naturally appear throughout the dungeon: signs, maps, spell
   books, potion recipes, treasure clues, character names, door inscriptions, quest
   instructions. Start with individual words, then short phrases, simple sentences,
   multi-step instructions, and eventually short passages and story comprehension.
6. **Colors.** Colors should be integrated into the environment. Examples: "Find the BLUE
   crystal." "Put the RED key into the RED door." "Find the small YELLOW potion." Eventually
   combine color + size + shape + instruction, e.g. "Bring me the small green crystal."
7. **Map reading.** Map reading should become a major game mechanic. Start with simple
   visual maps, e.g. Castle → Blue Room → Green Room → Treasure. The child learns spatial
   relationships, direction, sequencing, visual representation, navigation, memory and
   problem solving. Progression: (1) find a matching room, (2) follow one direction,
   (3) follow multiple directions, (4) use a simple dungeon map, (5) use
   north/south/east/west, (6) remember a route, (7) plan a route around obstacles.
8. **Memory & attention.** Example: the player enters a room. Three doors appear:
   🔴 🟢 🔵. Narrator: "Remember the GREEN door." Doors disappear. Player selects the
   correct door. Later: remember a sequence 🔵 → 🟡 → 🔴. Eventually combine memory with
   navigation and story clues.

## 🧪 The magic potion system

This should become one of the major learning mechanics. The adventurer discovers an
alchemist's workshop: a cauldron, ingredients, magical tools, and recipes. The player must
follow a recipe to create potions.

Example: POTION OF DRAGON COURAGE

1. Add 2 green leaves.
2. Add 1 blue crystal.
3. Add 3 red berries.
4. Stir 4 times.

The player must identify colors, count ingredients, drag ingredients, follow the sequence,
follow quantities, stir, and observe the result. When completed: POOF! The potion is
created.

The potion then becomes useful in the actual dungeon. Example: "The stone door is too heavy
to open." The player uses a Potion of Giant Strength. The character grows enormous and
opens the door. Learning has changed the game world.

### Potion difficulty progression

1. "Add red berry. Add blue crystal." Focus: color identification.
2. "Add 2 red berries. Add 1 yellow flower. Stir 3 times." Focus: counting + sequence.
3. "First add the blue crystal. Then add two red berries. Finally, stir five times." Focus:
   multi-step instructions.
4. Show the recipe briefly. Hide it. Child must remember the recipe. Focus: working memory.
5. Use language clues, e.g. "I need something green, something that sparkles blue, and
   three things that grow on bushes." Focus: reading comprehension + vocabulary + reasoning.

## Story campaigns

### Campaign 1: The Dragon's Prisoner

Classic fantasy quest. A princess has disappeared. A dragon is blamed. The kingdom's
knights believe the dragon has taken her. The player enters the dragon's dungeon to rescue
her. But the truth is more complicated than the kingdom believes.

Introduces: exploration, courage, basic dungeon mechanics, dice, math, colors, patterns,
potions, early reading.

### Campaign 2: The Dragon Who Didn't Want to Fight

The kingdom believes a terrible dragon has been stealing treasure. The player enters the
dragon's lair. Instead of finding a terrifying monster, they discover a lonely dragon. The
dragon has been collecting objects because it doesn't know how to make friends. Something
else is living deeper inside the dungeon. The player has to decide whether to help the
dragon.

Emphasizes: choices, empathy, storytelling, cause and effect, problem solving, social
reasoning. The game should not necessarily tell the child which choice is "morally
correct." Choices can create different story consequences.

### Campaign 3: The Princess's Quest

The kingdom's knights are preparing to rescue Princess Ruby. But Ruby isn't waiting to be
rescued. She has escaped the tower herself. She discovered that the dungeon beneath the
castle is changing. Doors are appearing where they shouldn't. Ancient rooms are waking.
Something beneath the kingdom is trying to rewrite the magical Storybook. Ruby becomes the
adventurer. The child adventures alongside her.

Emphasizes: independence, curiosity, problem solving, exploration, storytelling, more
advanced learning challenges.

## The world

Instead of mixing dinosaurs, pirates, astronauts, etc., the game should have a coherent
original fantasy world. Potential regions: 🌲 Whispering Woods, 🏰 Forgotten Castle,
🔥 Dragon Caverns, 🧙 Wizard's Tower, 💀 Bone Crypt, 🗺️ Ancient Ruins, 🌋 Ember Mountain,
👑 Lost Kingdom. Each area can introduce different mechanics while remaining part of one
connected world.

## Narration

The narrator is extremely important. The narrator should make the game feel like an
adventure rather than an educational app.

Never simply say "Correct!" Instead: "You got it! The ancient door begins to shake..." Or:
"Hmm... that wasn't quite right." "But wait!" "The wizard has an idea."

The narrator should react to correct answers, incorrect answers, dice rolls, choices,
exploration, discoveries and character relationships. Eventually, the game could support
increasingly dynamic storytelling.

## Failure philosophy

Do not make mistakes feel like failure. A wrong answer should create a funny consequence, a
new clue, a different route, a small obstacle, a chance to try again, or a story event.

The child should feel "Something happened!" rather than "I'm bad at this."

## Character progression

As children play, they gain experience, items, magical objects, new abilities, character
upgrades, new dungeon areas, story discoveries and companions. Learning progress can unlock
game progression, but avoid making the system feel like school grades. The child should
primarily experience: Adventure → Discovery → Growth.

## Adaptive learning

Architecture should separate the Narrative Engine, the Learning Engine, Game Mechanics and
the Progression System. This is important.

The same story event should eventually be able to generate different learning challenges
based on the child's ability. Example: a magical door requires a pattern. For one child:
🔴 🔵 🔴 ? For another: 🔴 🔵 🟢 🔴 🔵 ? For an advanced child: 🔴 🔵 🔵 🟢 🔴 🔵 🔵 ?
Same story. Different learning difficulty. The child should never feel like they have been
placed into a different "grade."

## Future machine learning

Do NOT make ML necessary for the first version. Build the data architecture so it can
support it later. Initially use deterministic rules.

Track things such as challenge type, difficulty, attempts, accuracy, time, hints used,
repeated mistakes, successful streaks, fine-motor interaction patterns, reading progress,
math progress, pattern difficulty and memory performance.

Eventually a tiny on-device ML model could estimate "This child is ready for more complex
addition" or "This child is strong at color matching but needs more practice with letter
sounds," then dynamically select challenges. The ML system should adapt the adventure, not
announce that the child is being tested.

## Android-first design

Build specifically for Android tablets/phones initially. Important considerations:
touch-first interaction, large touch targets, simple gestures, minimal typing, optional
audio narration, offline-first gameplay where practical, child-friendly UI, parent settings
separated from child gameplay, local progress storage, eventually optional cloud backup,
accessibility considerations, and multiple child profiles eventually.

The UI should feel like a fantasy game rather than an educational dashboard.

## Art direction

Create an original visual identity. Potential style: warm illustrated fantasy, friendly
monsters, expressive characters, cozy dungeon environments, magical lighting, hand-painted
storybook feeling, strong silhouettes, bright but not overstimulating colors.

Avoid directly imitating existing D&D artwork or other recognizable fantasy franchises.
Image generation can be used during development to prototype characters, dungeon rooms,
maps, monsters, potions, items, backgrounds and story illustrations. Final production
assets should be reviewed for consistency and originality.

## Core design principle

The most important rule for the entire project:

> The child should feel like they are playing an adventure game. We should know they are
> learning. They should not feel like they are doing schoolwork.

A math problem should open a dungeon door. A tracing exercise should cast a spell. A color
activity should identify a potion ingredient. A pattern should unlock an ancient mechanism.
A reading challenge should reveal a clue. A map activity should guide the party. A
storytelling choice should change what happens next. A dice roll should make the outcome
exciting. And the child's progress should gradually make them a more capable adventurer.

## First prototype

Do NOT build the entire game initially. Build one small vertical slice: THE FIRST DUNGEON.

Include:

1. Character selection
2. One dungeon map
3. 3–5 rooms
4. Basic narrator
5. Dice roll mechanic
6. Counting/addition challenge
7. Color challenge
8. Pattern challenge
9. Pre-writing/tracing challenge
10. Letter/phonics challenge
11. Simple map challenge
12. One potion recipe
13. One NPC
14. One meaningful story choice
15. One reward
16. One mini-boss/final challenge

The goal of the prototype is not content volume. The goal is proving: "Does it actually
feel like an adventure while the child is learning?" If that loop feels magical, then build
the world outward from there.
